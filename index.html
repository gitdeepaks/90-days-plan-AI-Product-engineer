Rules that keep you out of confusion

  • Only do what today says. New course, new sheet, new YouTube series: no, until 12 Jan.
  • DSA: 20 minutes alone before any hint. Brute force first, then the pattern.
  • After every problem, a 3-line note in the DSA sheet: pattern, invariant, mistake. The full notebook template is only for problems that land in WEAK.
  • No-AI and with-AI practice never mix. Monday to Thursday coding: no AI at all. Friday: with AI, and steering it well is the skill.
  • Saturday: one 50-minute timed mock, scored on the rubric in the Interview map tab. A human interviewer at least every other week.
  • Every week ships one visible thing: a demo, a note, a PR or a diagram.
  • Missing a day means you continue tomorrow, not restart. Sunday after 3 PM is off.

How a revision works

L1Recall, 2 min. Title only: say the pattern, the invariant and the approach out loud. L2Sketch, 5 min. Write the core lines from memory, then check your note. L3Full solve, 20 min. Blank editor, timer on, full code and a dry run.

After you solve a problem: Day 1 L1, Day 3 L2, Day 7 L1, Day 14 L2, Day 30 L3. Forget one and it goes to Weak, and its next revision is a full L3 solve the next day.

What the research says, in one paragraph

None of these five companies is a pure LeetCode shop. They test practical coding (build something, then extend it), React or full-stack builds, system design with an AI flavour, and deep conversations about what you've shipped. DSA still matters as the base, and ElevenLabs and Sarvam do ask it directly. The new thing is the AI-assisted round: some companies ban AI in interviews, some now hand you one and grade how you steer it. This plan practises both.

The AI-assisted round

Read a working codebase, fix planted bugs, extend it, then make it survive 100x the input, with an assistant beside you. You're graded on how precisely you steer it, not on whether tests go green.

  • Name the class and approach in your prompt. "Keep a running balance per account in a map" beats pasting the problem.
  • Read the code yourself first. Explaining the codebase in your own words is often a scored step.
  • Write pseudo-code as comments before prompting, so the assistant can't wander off.
  • Always run the code. Judge what comes back before accepting it.
  • Expect a no-AI follow-up on the same bug: database internals, locks, deadlocks, failure modes.
  • In the plan: every Friday at 12:30, alternating with code review and TypeScript types.
